2. Common Interview Questions

Q1: Why do BMS current measurements usually use a ΔΣ ADC rather than a SAR?
Answer outline: (1) Dynamic range: across a 100 µΩ shunt, parked current gives a few µV while cranking gives hundreds of mV; ΔΣ reaches high resolution through oversampling and noise shaping, and the HVPA extends range with AGC gain 1–512. (2) Noise immunity: the ΔΣ decimation filter is itself a low-pass with nulls that can be placed on interferers, while a SAR samples one instant and is more sensitive to spikes. (3) The cost is latency and bandwidth, but battery sensing only needs kSPS rates.
Q2: How does a window watchdog differ from a challenge-response watchdog?
Answer outline: a window watchdog only checks service timing, flagging service that is too early or too late; code stuck in a loop that contains the service instruction can still land inside the window. A challenge-response watchdog also requires the correct key sequence, usually tied to safety-task results, proving the tasks actually ran. The HVPA CRWDT resets or interrupts on too soon, too late or wrong key.
Q3: How does an automotive MCU cope with load dump?
Answer outline: (1) Standard: ISO 16750-2 Test A (unsuppressed, 79–101 V for 12 V) and Test B (suppressed in the alternator, commonly 35 V). (2) Layered protection: alternator suppression → module TVS, series resistor and filter capacitor → chip absolute maximum. The HVPA VBAT absolute maximum is 42 V, and the datasheet notes external protection may be required. (3) On-chip clamps suit ESD and short transients; load-dump energy must be absorbed externally.
Q4: What is a wettable-flank QFN and why do OEMs require it?
Answer outline: the sawn sides of a standard QFN are bare copper, so solder wets only the bottom pad and AOI cannot see the joint from above. A wettable flank adds a plated step on each lead’s side, solder climbs it and forms a visible fillet, so the line can inspect joints with AOI — part of the zero-defect requirement. The HVPA uses a 32-QFN wettable-flank package.
