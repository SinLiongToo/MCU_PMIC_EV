Precision Analog & Power: Microvolt Sensing and a 42 V Battery Supply

An IBS must measure shunt signals from a few mA of parked current to over a thousand amps of cranking current, on one chip that runs straight from the battery. Using the PACSS analog subsystem of the Infineon PSoC™ 4 HVPA-144K, this chapter follows the signal path: Kelvin wiring and filtering → chopper PGA → ΔΣ modulator → AGC and scaler → decimation chain, and finally the 42 V-class high-voltage regulator.

Device figures: PSoC™ 4 HVPA-144K datasheet 002-28660 Rev. *K (“DS”). Transistor-level details in the figures are teaching concepts, not Infineon’s published circuits.

1. Four-Terminal Kelvin Sensing & RC Anti-Alias Filter

Automotive shunts are typically only 100 µΩ. Solder and trace resistance can exceed the shunt itself, and copper has a temperature coefficient of about 3900 ppm/°C, so sense lines that share the high-current path corrupt the reading. A four-terminal Kelvin connection is mandatory: high current flows through the outer terminals and the sense lines tap the resistor body directly.

Filter corner frequency

The differential capacitor \(C_{\text{diff}}\) sees \(2R_f\), and the two common-mode capacitors appear as \(C_{\text{cm}}/2\) in series for differential signals: \[f_{c,\text{diff}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \cdot 2R_f \cdot \left(C_{\text{diff}} + \tfrac{C_{\text{cm}}}{2}\right)}\] With \(R_f = 100\,\Omega\), \(C_{\text{diff}} = 100\,\text{nF}\), \(C_{\text{cm}} = 10\,\text{nF}\): \[f_{c,\text{diff}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 200 \times 105\,\text{nF}} \approx 7.6\,\text{kHz}\]
  • 7.6 kHz keeps the current dynamics (the DS notes 8 kSPS is enough on the main channels for lead-acid) while giving first-order attenuation of PWM noise above 20 kHz.
  • Match both \(R_f\) (0.1% recommended) and keep \(C_{\text{diff}} \ge 10\,C_{\text{cm}}\) so asymmetry does not turn common-mode noise into a differential error.
  • The chip also has an on-chip anti-alias filter of about 30 kHz bandwidth (DS §1.8.1); the external RC mainly handles faster transients and ESD.

2. Chopper-Stabilised PGA

A 15 mA parked current across 100 µΩ is only 1.5 µV. Ordinary CMOS amplifiers have hundreds of µV of input offset plus low-frequency 1/f noise, enough to bury the signal. The PACSS analog channels include choppers to minimise offset (DS §1.8: “The choppers minimize offset error”).

  1. Modulate (CHOP 1): the input polarity alternates at \(f_{\text{ch}}\), moving the DC signal up to \(f_{\text{ch}}\).
  2. Amplify: the amplifier’s own offset \(V_{os}\) and 1/f noise stay near DC.
  3. Demodulate (CHOP 2): a second synchronous swap returns the signal to DC and moves \(V_{os}\) and 1/f noise up to \(f_{\text{ch}}\), where the digital filter removes them.
What chopping cannot fix: it removes only errors inside the amplifier. Thermo-EMF at the shunt (Seebeck) and voltage drops from asymmetric wiring are real input voltages that chopping cannot distinguish; they need symmetric layout and zero-current calibration. See Case 3.

3. ΔΣ Modulator

Each ADC consists of a PGA, anti-alias filter, buffer amplifier and a multi-level ΔΣ modulator (DS §1.8.1). The datasheet says continuous conversion needs a Sinc³ decimator “one order higher than the modulator”, which implies a second-order modulator. The figure sketches a second-order loop with switched-capacitor integrators.

Output of a second-order modulator in the z-domain: \[Y(z) = z^{-2}X(z) + (1 - z^{-1})^2 E(z)\] The noise transfer function \(\text{NTF}(z) = (1 - z^{-1})^2\) gives \(|\text{NTF}(e^{j\omega})| \approx \omega^2\) at low frequency: quantisation noise is pushed to high frequency, where the decimator removes it. An ideal second-order loop gains about 15 dB of SNR per doubling of OSR; a multi-level quantiser lowers quantisation noise further and improves stability.

4. AGC & Scaler: from mA to kA

The current-channel gain spans 1–512. The AGC watches the input with comparators and, when a threshold is crossed, a lookup table sets the PGA, modulator gain and scaler together (DS §1.8.5). The scaler multiplies results by 512/G so every LSB has the same weight: 0.715 mA with a 100 µΩ shunt.

5. Decimation Chain: Sinc³ → Sinc² → FIR

The modulator output passes through a Sinc³ decimator (ratio DR), a Sinc² decimator (ratio DR2) and a programmable 16-tap FIR, followed by compensation, averaging/accumulation and window comparison (DS §1.8.3–1.8.4).

  • Sinc³ nulls sit at \(N \cdot f_{\text{mod}}/DR\); Sinc² nulls at \(N \cdot f_{\text{mod}}/(DR \cdot DR2)\). DS example: OSR 64 at 3.072 MHz → 48 kHz; DR2 = 48 → nulls every 1 kHz.
  • DS: lead-acid applications need only 8 kSPS on the main voltage and current channels, leaving the third digital channel for temperature or diagnostics.
  • To reject both 50 Hz and 60 Hz mains, the averaging window must be a multiple of 100 ms (nulls at multiples of 10 Hz). Modulator clock and OSR alone give kHz-level nulls; they do not line up with 50/60 Hz by themselves.

Adjust DR, DR2 and averaging length in the MCU Lab and watch the nulls move.

6. High-Voltage Regulator & Bandgap Reference (Concept)

The HVPA runs straight from a 12 V or 24 V battery: VBAT operates from 3.6 to 28 V and remains functional up to 42 V (DS §1.2.1). The HV regulator produces 3.3 V VDDD and VDDA, which may drop to 2.7 V when VBAT falls below 4 V; VDDD can also feed external loads up to 10 mA. Core regulators then produce the 1.8 V VCCD.

  • Bandgap: a PTAT term \(V_T = kT/q\) is added to the CTAT \(V_{BE}\) to give a reference of about 1.2 V with a very low temperature coefficient: \(V_{\text{BG}} = V_{BE} + K \cdot V_T\).
  • Regulation loop: an error amplifier compares the divided output with \(V_{\text{BG}}\) and drives the HV pass device to hold 3.3 V.
  • External parts: DS Figure 1 calls for a series R1 (10–15 Ω) and C1 (2.2 µF) filter on VBAT plus an external reverse-polarity diode; VDDD, VDDA and VCCD each need X7R bypass capacitors.
  • Over-voltage: 42 V is the absolute maximum. The DS notes that external protection may be required (series resistor, diode, TVS) against high-voltage pulses. See Chapter 04.
