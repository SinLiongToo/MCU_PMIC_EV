High-Voltage Silicon Process: Monolithic Integration & Over-Voltage Protection

Putting a 1.8 V digital core, microvolt-level analog and 42 V-capable devices on one die needs a mixed-signal process with high-voltage devices (for example BCD: Bipolar-CMOS-DMOS), plus over-voltage protection planned at system level. This chapter covers where load dump comes from, the test standard, protection layering, and isolation and thermal aspects of the process.

Device figures: PSoC™ 4 HVPA-144K datasheet 002-28660 Rev. *K (“DS”). Infineon does not publish the HVPA process node or internal clamp design; transistor-level content here describes general principles.

1. Load Dump & Layered Over-Voltage Protection

A load dump happens when the battery is disconnected while the alternator is charging: the alternator field cannot collapse instantly, so its output rises sharply for hundreds of milliseconds. The current standard is ISO 16750-2 (the old ISO 7637-2:2004 pulses 5a/5b moved there).

The HVPA VBAT operates from 3.6 to 28 V, stays functional up to 42 V, and has an absolute maximum of 42 V (DS SIDA5); the LIN pin is rated −27 V to +42 V. Note 2 of the absolute maximum ratings says: “To prevent damage caused by high-voltage pulses, external protection (that is, series resistor, diode, TVS) may be required.” The right design approach is layered protection, not relying on an on-chip clamp to replace a TVS:

The chip still contains ESD and transient clamp structures. The figure shows a common RC/Zener-triggered active clamp: a Zener or RC network detects over-voltage or a fast dV/dt and turns on a large transistor that steers the energy to ground.

  • Static trigger: when the voltage slowly rises past the trigger point, Zener current builds a voltage across \(R_{\text{trig}}\) and turns on the clamp transistor.
  • dV/dt trigger: a very fast transient injects displacement current through \(C_{\text{trig}}\), turning the clamp on before the voltage reaches device limits.
  • Energy limit: on-chip clamps have limited area and energy capability — fine for ESD and short transients. Load-dump energy (hundreds of ms, tens of joules) must be absorbed externally. Estimate TVS energy in the MCU Lab.

An LDMOS spreads the electric field over a long, lightly doped drift region to raise breakdown voltage, at the cost of on-resistance and area. Since the HVPA’s VBAT, LIN and VSENSE pins must withstand 42 V, those paths use high-voltage devices.

3. Isolation: Deep Trench Isolation (DTI) & Substrate Noise

High-voltage switching injects minority carriers and displacement current into the shared substrate, which can travel to microvolt-level analog circuits or trigger parasitic latch-up. Common countermeasures are deep trench isolation (DTI), guard rings, buried layers, and floor-planning that separates HV and analog areas with separate ground returns. The HVPA’s requirement for external 2.2 kΩ series resistors on the VSENSE/VDIAG high-voltage sense inputs, to limit ESD and transient current (DS), follows the same logic.
